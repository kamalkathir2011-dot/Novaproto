1. The Universe and Space
The universe is all of space, time, matter and energy. Current measurements put its age at about 13.8
billion years, beginning with the Big Bang, a rapid expansion from an extremely hot, dense state. The
universe is still expanding, and the expansion is accelerating, an effect attributed to dark energy. Ordinary
matter makes up only about 5% of the universe; roughly 27% is dark matter and 68% is dark energy.
• Galaxy: a huge system of stars, gas, dust and dark matter held together by gravity. Our galaxy, the
  Milky Way, holds hundreds of billions of stars.
• Star: a ball of plasma that shines by nuclear fusion. The Sun is an average star about 4.6 billion years
  old.
• Light-year: the distance light travels in a year, about 9.46 trillion km. Light moves at about 299,792 km
  per second.
• Black hole: a region where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape.

Beyond Neptune lie the Kuiper Belt and dwarf planets such as Pluto. The Moon orbits Earth about every
27 days and causes most ocean tides together with the Sun.


2. Planet Earth
Earth formed about 4.54 billion years ago. It has a diameter of about 12,742 km, and roughly 71% of its
surface is covered by water.

Structure
• Crust: thin outer rock layer, split into tectonic plates.
• Mantle: thick layer of hot, slowly flowing rock.
• Outer core: liquid iron and nickel, which generates Earth's magnetic field.
• Inner core: solid iron-nickel ball, extremely hot and under immense pressure.
Plate tectonics explains earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain building and the slow movement of
continents.

Atmosphere, Water and Climate
Dry air is about 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, 0.9% argon and a trace of carbon dioxide (about 0.04%).
Weather is short-term atmospheric conditions; climate is the long-term average. Greenhouse gases such
as carbon dioxide and methane trap heat, and human activity has raised their levels since the Industrial
Revolution, causing global warming. The water cycle moves water through evaporation, condensation,
precipitation and runoff.

Records and Features
• Seven continents: Africa, Antarctica, Asia, Australia/Oceania, Europe, North America, South America.
• Five oceans: Pacific (largest), Atlantic, Indian, Southern, Arctic.
• Highest mountain above sea level: Mount Everest, about 8,849 m. Deepest point: Mariana Trench,
  about 10,900 m below sea level.
• The Nile and the Amazon are the two longest rivers; which is longest depends on how rivers are
  measured.
• Largest hot desert: Sahara. Largest desert overall: Antarctica.


3. Life and Biology
Life is organized matter that grows, reproduces, responds to its environment, uses energy and evolves.
The basic unit of life is the cell. DNA carries genetic instructions as a code written in four chemical bases
(A, T, C, G); genes are segments of DNA that guide the making of proteins.
• Evolution by natural selection: inherited traits that help survival and reproduction become more
  common over generations. Life on Earth began at least 3.5 billion years ago.
• Three domains of life: Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya (which includes animals, plants, fungi and
  protists).
• Taxonomy order: domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species.
• Photosynthesis: plants, algae and some bacteria convert light, water and carbon dioxide into sugar
  and oxygen.


• Ecosystem: a community of organisms interacting with each other and their environment. Food chains
  link producers, consumers and decomposers.
• Scientists have described roughly 2 million species; estimates of the total number run into the millions.

Major Animal Groups
Invertebrates (insects, mollusks, worms, spiders and many more) make up the vast majority of animal
species. Vertebrates include fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals. Mammals are warm-blooded,
have hair or fur and nurse their young with milk.


4. The Human Body and Health
The adult human skeleton has 206 bones. The body is made of an estimated 30 to 40 trillion cells
organized into tissues, organs and systems.

  System                    Main role

  Circulatory               Heart and blood vessels move blood, oxygen and nutrients

  Respiratory               Lungs exchange oxygen and carbon dioxide

  Digestive                 Breaks down food and absorbs nutrients

  Nervous                   Brain, spinal cord and nerves control and sense

  Immune                    Defends against infection

  Endocrine                 Hormones regulate growth, metabolism, mood

  Musculoskeletal           Support and movement

  Excretory/Urinary         Kidneys filter blood and remove waste

  Reproductive              Produces offspring

  Integumentary             Skin, hair and nails; the skin is the largest organ

• Infectious diseases are caused by germs such as bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites. Vaccines
  train the immune system to recognize them; antibiotics treat bacterial infections only.
• Healthy living basics: balanced diet, regular physical activity, adequate sleep, avoiding tobacco, limiting
  alcohol and keeping up with medical checkups.
• This primer is general information, not medical advice.


5. Matter, Chemistry and Physics
Chemistry
Matter is anything with mass that takes up space. It is made of atoms, each with a nucleus of protons and
neutrons surrounded by electrons. The periodic table organizes the 118 known elements by atomic
number (number of protons). A molecule is two or more atoms bonded together, for example water (H2O)
and carbon dioxide (CO2). The common states of matter are solid, liquid, gas and plasma. The pH scale
measures acidity: below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral, above 7 is basic.

Physics
• Newton's laws: (1) objects keep their motion unless a force acts; (2) force equals mass times
  acceleration; (3) every action has an equal and opposite reaction.


• Four fundamental forces: gravity, electromagnetism, the strong nuclear force and the weak nuclear
  force.
• Energy is conserved: it changes form (kinetic, potential, thermal, chemical, electrical, nuclear) but is
  not created or destroyed.
• Thermodynamics: heat flows from hot to cold, and the disorder (entropy) of an isolated system tends
  to increase.
• Relativity (Einstein) links space and time and shows that mass and energy are equivalent: E = mc2.
• Quantum mechanics describes the behavior of matter and energy at the smallest scales, where
  particles also behave like waves.


6. Mathematics
• Number types: natural (1, 2, 3...), integers (including negatives and zero), rationals (fractions),
  irrationals (such as pi and the square root of 2), and real numbers.
• Arithmetic uses addition, subtraction, multiplication and division. A prime number has exactly two
  divisors: 1 and itself.
• Algebra uses symbols for unknown quantities. Geometry studies shapes and space. Calculus studies
  change and accumulation. Statistics studies data and uncertainty; probability measures how likely
  events are.
• Pythagorean theorem: in a right triangle, a2 + b2 = c2, where c is the longest side.
• Pi is the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter, about 3.14159. Circle area = pi x r2.
• The metric system uses powers of ten: kilo (1,000), centi (1/100), milli (1/1,000).


7. Geography and Countries
The world population is roughly 8.2 billion. There are 193 United Nations member states, plus two
observer states (the Holy See and Palestine).

  Largest economies           United States, China, Germany, Japan, India

• Latitude measures distance north or south of the equator; longitude measures distance east or west

• Time zones divide the world into regions that share the same standard time, generally offset from UTC.

9. Government, Law and Economics
Government and Law
• Democracy: power rests with the people, who vote directly or elect representatives. Monarchy: a
  hereditary ruler (often constitutional today). Republic: a state with elected leadership rather than a
  monarch. Authoritarianism: power concentrated in one person or group.
• Separation of powers: dividing government into legislative (makes laws), executive (carries them out)
  and judicial (interprets them) branches.
• Human rights are basic rights held by every person; the Universal Declaration of Human Rights was
  adopted by the UN in 1948.
• Two main legal traditions: common law (based on precedent) and civil law (based on codes).

Economics
• Scarcity is the basic problem: wants are unlimited, resources are limited.
• Supply and demand: prices tend to rise when demand exceeds supply and fall when supply exceeds
  demand.
• GDP measures the total value of goods and services a country produces. Inflation is a general rise in
  prices over time.
• Money serves as a medium of exchange, a unit of account and a store of value.
• Major systems: market economies (private ownership), planned economies (state control) and mixed
  economies, which most countries use.


10. Language, Religion and Philosophy
Language


About 7,000 languages are spoken today, and many are endangered. Mandarin Chinese has the most
native speakers; English is the most widely spoken language overall when second-language speakers are
counted. Other major languages include Spanish, Hindi, Arabic, French, Bengali, Portuguese, Russian and
Urdu. Languages are grouped into families, such as Indo-European, Sino-Tibetan, Afro-Asiatic and
Niger-Congo.

Religion
Christianity and Islam are the two largest religions by followers, followed by Hinduism and Buddhism.
Others include Judaism, Sikhism, Taoism, Shinto, many indigenous traditions, and a large and growing
group of people with no religious affiliation.

Philosophy
• Metaphysics: the nature of reality. Epistemology: the nature of knowledge. Ethics: how we ought to
  act. Logic: the principles of valid reasoning. Aesthetics: the nature of beauty and art.
• Influential thinkers include Socrates, Plato, Aristotle, Confucius, Laozi, Avicenna, Aquinas, Descartes,
  Kant and Nietzsche.


11. Arts and Culture
• Visual arts: painting, sculpture, photography, architecture and film. Performing arts: music, dance
  and theater. Literature: poetry, drama, fiction and nonfiction.
• Major art movements in the West include Renaissance, Baroque, Romanticism, Impressionism, Cubism
  and Surrealism; every world region has its own rich traditions.
• Music: built from rhythm, melody and harmony; classical, jazz, folk, rock, hip-hop and electronic music
  are among the many genres.
• Sport: association football (soccer) is the most popular sport worldwide; the Olympic Games are held
  every four years, with the FIFA World Cup also on a four-year cycle.


12. Technology, Energy and Food
Technology
• Computer: a machine that processes data using instructions (software). Information is stored as bits (0
  and 1).
• Internet: a global network of networks; the World Wide Web is the system of linked pages that runs on
  it, invented around 1989-1991.
• Artificial intelligence is software that performs tasks that normally require human intelligence;
  machine learning trains systems from data.
• Other key technologies: electricity, the printing press, antibiotics, engines, aircraft, satellites,
  semiconductors, the smartphone and renewable energy.

Energy
Most of the world's energy still comes from fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas). Low-carbon sources include
solar, wind, hydroelectric, geothermal and nuclear power; solar and wind are growing fastest.

Food and Agriculture


Staple crops that feed much of humanity are rice, wheat, maize (corn) and potatoes. Humans need
macronutrients (carbohydrates, proteins, fats) and micronutrients (vitamins and minerals), plus water.
Agriculture began about 10,000 years ago and today uses roughly half of the world's habitable land.

PART I: CINEMA
What Is Cinema?
Cinema is the art and industry of moving images, usually combined with sound. A film works because of
persistence of vision and the phi phenomenon: when still images are shown in quick succession
(traditionally 24 frames per second), the brain perceives smooth motion. Cinema blends photography,
drama, music, design, writing and editing, which is why it is often called the 'seventh art'.

The Language of Film
• Shot sizes: extreme long shot, long shot, medium shot, close-up and extreme close-up, each
  controlling how much we see and how close we feel.
• Camera angles and movement: high and low angles, tilt, pan, tracking (dolly), crane, handheld and
  steadicam shots.
• Mise-en-scene: everything placed in the frame: sets, costumes, props, lighting, actors' positions and
  color palette.


• Editing: the joining of shots. Common tools are the cut, the match cut, cross-cutting (parallel action),
  the fade, the dissolve and montage.
• Sound: dialogue, sound effects, ambient sound and music; diegetic sound comes from within the story
  world, non-diegetic (such as a score) does not.
• Cinematography: lens choice, depth of field, framing, exposure and lighting. Aspect ratio is the shape
  of the frame, such as 1.85:1 or 2.39:1.

Stages of production: development (idea, script, funding), pre-production (casting, planning, locations),

Genres
• Drama, comedy, romance: the broad foundations of storytelling on screen.
• Action, adventure, thriller, crime, war, western: story types built around conflict and tension.
• Horror, science fiction, fantasy: genres of the imagination, often used to explore real fears and ideas.
• Musical, animation, family: animation is a technique rather than a genre, and covers everything from
  children's films to adult drama.
• Documentary: nonfiction film about real people and events. Experimental/avant-garde: film that
  breaks conventional narrative.

Animation and Technology


Animation ranges from hand-drawn (Snow White, 1937, the first full-length cel-animated feature) to
stop-motion, anime and computer-generated imagery (CGI). Film itself has moved from physical celluloid
to digital capture, and from silent black-and-white to color, surround sound, IMAX and streaming. Today's
films commonly combine live action, practical effects, motion capture and computer-generated worlds.

The Business
Film revenue comes from theaters, streaming, home entertainment, television and merchandise. The
highest-grossing film of all time is generally listed as Avatar (2009), with several superhero and franchise
films following. Many of the most-watched films in history are not recent: adjusted for inflation, Gone with
the Wind ranks among the top earners.

How to Watch Films Well
• Notice what is in the frame, how the camera moves and why the director cut when they did.
• Listen to the sound design and music, and ask what they add emotionally.
• Watch widely across eras, countries and genres, including silent films and documentaries.
• After watching, ask what the film is about beyond its plot.


PART II: LIVING BEINGS ON EARTH
What Is Life?
Living things share these features: they are made of cells, use energy (metabolism), grow and develop,
reproduce, respond to their surroundings, maintain internal balance (homeostasis), carry genetic
information and evolve over generations. Biology is the science of life. Life on Earth is thought to have
begun at least 3.5 billion years ago, and all known living things share a common ancestor.

Viruses are not usually counted as living, because they cannot reproduce on their own; they must use a
host cell.

Plants
• Bryophytes (mosses, liverworts): no true roots or vascular system; need moisture.
• Ferns and relatives: have vascular tissue; reproduce with spores.
• Gymnosperms (conifers, cycads, ginkgo): seed plants with 'naked' seeds, such as pine cones.
• Angiosperms: flowering plants, the largest and most diverse group with about 300,000 species. They
  include grasses, grains, fruit trees and most food crops.
• Records: coast redwoods are the tallest trees (over 110 m). The General Sherman giant sequoia is the
  largest tree by volume. Some bristlecone pines are around 4,000 to 5,000 years old. The
  quaking-aspen colony 'Pando' in Utah is a single clonal organism spread over about 40 hectares.
Plants produce oxygen, store carbon, feed nearly all land animals, stabilize soil and regulate water. By
weight, plants make up around 80% of all living biomass.

placentals (the majority).

Animal Records
• Largest animal ever: the blue whale (up to about 30 m long and 150+ tonnes). Largest land animal:
  the African bush elephant.
• Fastest animal: the peregrine falcon in a dive (over 300 km/h). Fastest on land: the cheetah (about
  100 km/h in short bursts).
• Tallest: the giraffe (up to about 5.5 m). Longest migration: the Arctic tern, flying pole to pole each
  year.


• Deep divers: Cuvier's beaked whale holds the mammal dive record of nearly 3,000 m.
• Long-lived: the Greenland shark may live 250 to 500 years; the ocean quahog clam over 500 years.

How Living Things Interact
• Food webs: producers (plants, algae) are eaten by herbivores, which are eaten by carnivores;
  decomposers (fungi, bacteria) recycle nutrients.
• Predation, competition, mutualism, parasitism: the basic relationships between species. Bees and
  flowers are a classic mutualism.
• Symbiosis in practice: coral polyps host algae; lichens combine fungi with algae; our own gut hosts
  trillions of microbes.
• Keystone species have an outsized effect on their ecosystem, such as sea otters, wolves and
  beavers.
• Intelligence and behavior: many animals use tools, communicate and learn, including crows,
  octopuses, dolphins, elephants and great apes.

Biomes: Where Life Lives
  Biome                          Features

  Tropical rainforest            Warm and wet year-round; richest biodiversity on land

  Savanna and grassland          Grasses with scattered trees; large herds of grazers

  Desert                         Very little rain; specialized plants and animals

  Temperate forest               Four seasons; deciduous and mixed forests

  Boreal forest (taiga)          Cold conifer forest across the far north

  Tundra and polar               Frozen ground or ice; short growing season

  Wetlands                       Marshes and swamps; nurseries for many species

                                 species

  Freshwater                     Rivers, lakes and ponds; home to a disproportionate share of species


Evolution and Heredity
• Natural selection (Darwin and Wallace, 1850s): organisms with traits that suit their environment tend
  to leave more offspring.
• Genetics: Gregor Mendel (1860s) discovered the basic rules of inheritance; DNA's double-helix
  structure was described in 1953.
• Mutation creates new variation; speciation forms new species when populations become isolated and
  diverge.
• Evidence for evolution includes fossils, comparative anatomy, shared DNA, and observed changes
  such as antibiotic resistance.
• Humans share about 98 to 99% of their DNA with chimpanzees, and a great deal with all living things.

Mass Extinctions and Today's Crisis
Life has survived five major mass extinctions. The largest, the end-Permian event about 252 million years
ago, eliminated the great majority of marine species. The most recent, about 66 million years ago, ended


the non-bird dinosaurs. Many scientists warn that we are in a sixth, human-driven wave of extinction.
• Main threats: habitat loss (especially for farming), overexploitation, invasive species, pollution and
  climate change.
• IUCN Red List categories: Least Concern, Near Threatened, Vulnerable, Endangered, Critically
  Endangered, Extinct in the Wild, Extinct.
• Conservation successes: recoveries of humpback whales, bald eagles, giant pandas (downlisted
  from Endangered to Vulnerable in 2016) and the Iberian lynx show that protection can work.
• What helps: protected areas, restoring habitat, sustainable fishing and farming, ending wildlife
  trafficking and reducing greenhouse gas emissions.

Humans among Living Beings
Humans are mammals, primates and great apes. Our species, Homo sapiens, is the only surviving
member of the genus Homo. We make up a tiny fraction of Earth's living mass (well under 1%), yet have
transformed the planet. Livestock now outweigh all wild mammals combined by a wide margin.

                       Cinema Actors
          The craft, the history, the stars and the business of screen acting


A broad reference guide. Records and rankings reflect well-established facts up to the

1. What Is Screen Acting?
An actor is a person who portrays a character in a performance. In cinema the actor performs for a
camera rather than a live audience, usually in small pieces filmed out of order over weeks or months. Film
acting is a collaboration: the director shapes the performance, the cinematographer frames it, and the
editor decides which moments the audience sees.

Stage vs. Screen
• Scale: theater actors project to the back row; film actors can work with a whisper or a glance because
  the camera captures tiny expressions in close-up.
• Continuity: scenes are shot many times from different angles, so an actor must repeat gestures and
  emotion precisely from take to take.
• Out of sequence: the last scene of a story is often filmed first, so actors must track their character's
  emotional journey in their heads.
• Control: a stage performance belongs to the actor each night; a film performance is finally shaped in
  the edit.

3. Acting Techniques and Training


  Approach                         Core idea

                                   through the character's goals (objectives), obstacles and given
                                   circumstances. The foundation of most modern training

  The Method (Strasberg)           Lee Strasberg at the Actors Studio stressed 'affective memory': using the
                                   actor's own past emotions to fuel a performance

  Stella Adler                     Emphasized imagination, social circumstances and text analysis over
                                   personal memory

                                   does in the moment ('listening')

Method acting in popular culture often means staying in character off set. Many acting teachers and
actors say this is not required, and some warn it can be unhealthy or disrespectful to colleagues; many
great performances come from technique, preparation and imagination.

How an Actor Prepares
• Script analysis: understanding the character's objective, relationships and arc.
• Research: studying real people, jobs, places and historical periods.
• Physical work: voice, accent, posture, movement, sometimes learning skills such as playing an
  instrument or a sport.
• Transformation: hair, makeup, costume, and sometimes weight change; actors increasingly discuss
  the health costs of extreme transformations.
• Rehearsal and read-throughs: common in film but usually shorter than for theater.
• On set: hitting marks, matching eye-lines, repeating takes, acting to a camera or to a tennis ball on a
  stick when effects will be added later.

others fight it by choosing contrasting parts. Chemistry is the visible connection between actors in a
scene, which casting directors try to test for.


5. The Star System and the Business of Acting
• Casting: casting directors find actors; actors audition (live or by recorded self-tape) and may do screen
  tests and chemistry reads.
• Agents and managers: represent actors, find work and negotiate contracts.
• Billing: the order and size of names in credits and posters, often contractually negotiated.
• Pay: ranges from minimum union scale to very large fees and profit participation for top stars. Most
  working actors earn modest and irregular incomes.
• Unions: SAG-AFTRA in the United States, Equity in the United Kingdom, and similar guilds elsewhere
  set minimum pay, safety rules and working conditions. In 2023 a major SAG-AFTRA strike, alongside
  the writers, lasted roughly four months and raised issues about streaming pay and artificial intelligence.
• Studio-era contracts: in classic Hollywood, studios held actors under multi-year contracts, controlled
  their images and often changed their names.

Modern Issues
• Representation and diversity: debate and progress around who gets cast and which stories are told.
• Safety and respect: intimacy coordinators, stunt safety, and better workplace conduct standards.
• Digital likeness and AI: consent and compensation for digital replicas of an actor's face and voice.
• Streaming: changed residuals, release patterns and the number of roles available.

Sound and light are both waves, and both shape how we experience art. This guide
   explains the science and the culture behind them. Figures are approximate.


PART I: SOUND
What Is Sound?
Sound is a vibration that travels as a wave through a medium such as air, water or a solid. A vibrating
object (a guitar string, a drum skin, vocal cords) pushes and pulls on the nearby particles, creating
alternating zones of compression and rarefaction that spread outward. Sound is a longitudinal
(compression) wave and cannot travel through a vacuum, which is why space is silent.

The Properties of a Sound Wave
  Property                 What it means                                     What we hear

  Frequency                Number of vibrations per second, in hertz         Pitch: high frequency = high note
                           (Hz)

  Amplitude                Size of the pressure variation                    Loudness (volume)

  Wavelength               Distance between repeating points of the          Linked to pitch and to how sound
                           wave; shorter for higher frequencies              bends around objects

  Waveform and             The shape of the wave, built from a               Timbre: the tone color that
  harmonics                fundamental plus overtones                        distinguishes a violin from a flute

  Duration and envelope    How a sound starts (attack), holds, and fades     Articulation: plucked, bowed,
                           (decay and release)                               sustained or short


Speed of Sound
  Medium                               Approximate speed

  Air at 20 C                          About 343 meters per second (about 1,235 km/h)

  Fresh water                          About 1,480 meters per second

  Steel                                About 5,000 to 6,000 meters per second

Sound moves faster in warmer air and much faster in liquids and solids because their particles are packed
closer together. Light is nearly a million times faster, which is why you see lightning before you hear
thunder.

Hearing
• Human range: roughly 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz in young, healthy ears. Below is infrasound (elephants and
  whales use it); above is ultrasound (bats and dolphins use it; also used in medical imaging).
• The ear: the outer ear collects sound; the eardrum vibrates; the three tiny bones of the middle ear
  (malleus, incus and stapes) amplify the vibrations; in the inner ear, the fluid-filled cochlea converts
  them into nerve signals through thousands of hair cells.
• Loudness is measured in decibels (dB), a logarithmic scale: every 10 dB is a tenfold increase in
  sound power. A whisper is about 30 dB, normal conversation about 60 dB, a lawnmower about 90 dB,
  and a loud concert about 110 dB. Long exposure above about 85 dB can damage hearing permanently.
• Direction: having two ears lets the brain locate sounds from tiny differences in arrival time and
  loudness.

Key Sound Phenomena


• Reflection and echo: sound bounces off hard surfaces; reverberation is many closely spaced
  reflections, important in concert halls.
• Resonance: an object vibrating strongly at its natural frequency. Instrument bodies, guitar boxes and
  organ pipes use it to amplify tone.
• Doppler effect: the pitch of a moving source rises as it approaches and falls as it leaves, as with a
  passing ambulance.
• Interference and beats: two close frequencies combine to produce a pulsing loudness (beats), which
  tuners use to tune instruments.
• Diffraction: low-frequency sounds bend around corners more easily, which is why bass is heard
  through walls.
• Noise cancellation: headphones generate a wave that cancels incoming sound.


PART II: MUSIC
What Is Music?
Music is the organization of sound in time for expression, communication, ritual or enjoyment. Every
known human culture makes music. Its basic building blocks are:

  Element                     Meaning

  Pitch                       How high or low a note sounds

  Rhythm and meter            Patterns of long and short sounds and how beats are grouped (for example, 4/4 or
                              3/4)

  Tempo                       Speed of the beat, measured in beats per minute (BPM)

  Melody                      A sequence of notes heard as a single musical idea

  Harmony                     Notes sounding together; chords and chord progressions

  Timbre                      The tone color of a voice or instrument

  Dynamics                    Loudness and its changes (soft: piano, loud: forte)

  Texture                     How layers combine: one line (monophonic), melody with accompaniment
                              (homophonic), or interwoven lines (polyphonic)

  Form                        The structure of a piece, such as verse-chorus, ABA, theme and variations,
                              sonata


The Science of Pitch
• Standard concert pitch sets the note A above middle C (A4) at 440 Hz. Middle C is about 261.6 Hz.
• An octave is a doubling of frequency: A4 at 440 Hz and A5 at 880 Hz sound like the same note, higher.
• Western music divides the octave into 12 semitones. In equal temperament each semitone multiplies
  the frequency by the twelfth root of two (about 1.0595).
• A harmonic series of overtones (2x, 3x, 4x the fundamental frequency, and so on) is present in most
  natural sounds and gives instruments their color.
• A consonant interval such as a perfect fifth has a simple frequency ratio of 3:2; an octave is 2:1.

Scales, Chords and Notation
• Scale: an ordered set of notes. The major scale sounds bright; the minor scale often sounds darker.
  The pentatonic scale has five notes and is found in music worldwide. The chromatic scale uses all 12
  semitones.
• Chord: three or more notes played together. A triad stacks root, third and fifth.
• Key is the home note and scale of a piece. Modulation is a change of key.
• Notation: Western staff notation places notes on five lines, using clefs, key and time signatures. Other
  systems include tablature for guitar, Indian sargam, Chinese jianpu and shape notes.
• Not all music uses Western tuning: Indian ragas, Arabic maqams and Indonesian gamelan use different
  scales and finer pitch inflections.

  Cello and double bass        Lower members of the string family. The cello has four strings tuned C-G-D-A; the
                               double bass is the deepest orchestral string


 Instrument                  Key facts

 Guitar                      Usually six strings, standard tuning E-A-D-G-B-E. Acoustic, classical and electric
                             forms. The electric guitar emerged in the 1930s and defined rock

 Flute                       Air split across an edge; modern concert flute is metal with keys. Flutes are
                             among the oldest instruments in the world

 Clarinet and oboe           Single-reed (clarinet) and double-reed (oboe) woodwinds

 Saxophone                   Invented by Adolphe Sax in the 1840s; a brass body with a single reed, important
                             in jazz

 Trumpet, trombone, tuba     Brass instruments; valves or a slide change the length of the tube and so the pitch

 Drum kit and percussion     Drums provide rhythm; tuned percussion such as marimba and vibraphone give
                             melody

                             are among the largest instruments

                             hands near two antennas

 Other world instruments     Sitar and tabla (India), oud (Middle East), erhu and guzheng (China), shamisen
                             and koto (Japan), kora (West Africa), didgeridoo (Australia), bagpipes (Scotland
                             and elsewhere), steel pan (Trinidad and Tobago)


Why Instruments Sound Different
• Every note an instrument plays contains a fundamental frequency plus overtones; the mix of overtones
  creates timbre.
• Materials, shape and size matter: longer strings and tubes give lower pitches.
• The attack (how a note begins) matters greatly; a piano note with its hammer strike sounds very
  different from a bowed violin note at the same pitch.
• Musicians produce vibrato, bends, slides and other expressions to shape the sound.

Making Music Together
• Ensembles: string quartet (two violins, viola, cello), jazz combo, rock band, choir, big band, brass
  band, orchestra.
• Tuning: ensemble members match a common reference pitch (usually A = 440 Hz; some period
  orchestras use lower pitches).
• Learning an instrument: practice, ear training and rhythm are the foundation; playing with others
  builds listening skills.


PART IV: LIGHT
What Is Light?
Light is electromagnetic radiation that our eyes can detect. It behaves both as a wave and as a stream of
particles called photons, a feature of quantum physics known as wave-particle duality. Unlike sound, light
needs no medium and travels through empty space at about 299,792 km per second. Sunlight takes
about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to reach Earth.

The Electromagnetic Spectrum
  Type                      Wavelength (approx.)               Examples and uses

  Radio waves               Meters to kilometers               Radio, television, Wi-Fi, radar

  Microwaves                Millimeters to centimeters         Ovens, satellite and mobile communication

  Infrared                  About 700 nm to 1 mm               Heat radiation, remote controls, night-vision
                                                               cameras

  Visible light             About 380 to 750 nm                What we see

  Ultraviolet               About 10 to 380 nm                 Causes sunburn and tanning; sterilization

  X-rays                    About 0.01 to 10 nm                Medical imaging

  Gamma rays                Less than 0.01 nm                  Produced by radioactive decay and cosmic events;
                                                               cancer therapy

All of these travel at the speed of light. The shorter the wavelength, the higher the frequency and the more
energy each photon carries.

Colors of Visible Light
  Color                              Approximate wavelength

  Red                                620 to 750 nm

  Orange                             590 to 620 nm

  Yellow                             570 to 590 nm

  Green                              495 to 570 nm

  Blue                               450 to 495 nm

  Violet                             380 to 450 nm

White light is a mixture of all visible colors. A prism or raindrops spread it into a spectrum, as in a rainbow,
because different colors bend by slightly different amounts when they enter glass or water.

How Light Behaves
• Reflection: light bounces off surfaces; mirrors give clear images. The angle in equals the angle out.
• Refraction: light changes direction when passing between materials, such as air and water; it makes a
  straw look bent and is how lenses work.
• Absorption: materials take in some wavelengths; a red apple reflects mostly red light and absorbs the
  rest.
• Scattering: the sky is blue because air molecules scatter short blue wavelengths more strongly than
  red ones (Rayleigh scattering); sunsets look red because light travels through more atmosphere.


• Diffraction and interference: light bends around edges and waves can combine, showing the wave
  nature of light and creating effects in soap bubbles and CDs.
• Polarization: light waves vibrating in one direction; used in sunglasses and 3D cinema.

Color Mixing
• Additive color (light): red, green and blue light combine to make white. Screens and projectors use this
  RGB system.
• Subtractive color (pigments): cyan, magenta, yellow (and black) inks absorb light and mix toward
  dark. Printers use this CMYK system. Traditional painting uses red, yellow and blue as practical
  primaries.

Seeing Light
• The eye focuses light with the cornea and lens onto the retina, which has about 120 million rods
  (dim-light, black-and-white vision) and about 6 million cones (bright light and color).
• Most people have three kinds of cones, tuned roughly to red, green and blue light; the brain compares
  their signals to create all colors we see. Color blindness usually comes from one cone type working
  differently.
• Some animals see differently: bees see ultraviolet, many birds have four cone types, and some snakes
  sense infrared heat.

Sources and Technology of Light
• Natural: the Sun, stars, fire, lightning and living organisms that glow (bioluminescence, such as fireflies
  and deep-sea fish).
• Artificial: incandescent bulbs (hot filament), fluorescent lamps, and LEDs, which are now the most
  efficient common lighting.
• Lasers (invented 1960) produce a narrow, coherent beam of one color, used in surgery,
  communication, barcode scanners and cutting.
• Fiber optics carry the internet as pulses of light through glass fibers.
• Lenses and instruments: eyeglasses, cameras, microscopes and telescopes; photography and
  cinema are literally 'writing with light'.
• Solar cells turn sunlight directly into electricity.


Where Sound and Light Meet
                              Sound                                        Light

 What it is                   Mechanical pressure wave in matter           Electromagnetic wave (and photons)

 Needs a medium?              Yes                                          No

 Speed                        About 343 m/s in air                         About 299,792 km/s in vacuum

 Wave type                    Longitudinal                                 Transverse

 Pitch or color               Frequency sets pitch                         Frequency (wavelength) sets color

 Strength                     Amplitude sets loudness                      Amplitude sets brightness

 Sensed by                    Ears (hair cells)                            Eyes (rods and cones)


• Both waves reflect, refract, diffract and interfere, so the same physics explains echoes and mirrors, and
  acoustic lenses and optical lenses.
• Synesthesia: in some people, sounds trigger colors; composers such as Scriabin and Messiaen are
  often associated with color-music ideas.
• Performance arts combine them: concerts with stage lighting, film with image and soundtrack, laser
  shows, and music videos.
• Spectra: both light and sound can be broken into frequencies (a prism for light, a spectrum analyzer or
  the ear for sound).
• Technology: ultrasound imaging, sonar, lidar and radar all use waves to 'see'; microphones and
  cameras are sound and light sensors.
